\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Drug-target interaction (DTI) prediction is a fundamental problem in computational drug discovery, because it connects candidate molecules with potential therapeutic targets before expensive biochemical validation. Reliable DTI models can accelerate chemical-genomics screening, target identification, and drug repositioning by narrowing the search space of plausible compound-protein pairs \citep{keiser2007relating,yamanishi2008prediction,liu2007bindingdb}. The task is intrinsically multimodal: interaction signals depend jointly on molecular chemistry, three-dimensional ligand geometry, protein sequence context, and the pair-specific compatibility of the two modalities. An effective model therefore must encode each side with appropriate inductive biases and preserve the interaction structure that distinguishes binding from non-binding pairs.

Computational DTI prediction has evolved through several methodological phases. Pre-deep-learning approaches framed the task as a similarity or network-inference problem, exploiting the heuristic that chemically related drugs tend to bind functionally related targets \citep{yamanishi2008prediction,keiser2007relating}; matrix-factorization and heterogeneous-network methods then formalized this view by recovering latent drug and target factors from sparse interaction matrices and broader biomedical relations \citep{liu2016nrlmf,he2017simboost,luo2017dtinet,wan2019neodti,ye2021kgenfm}, while classical machine learning supplied strong baselines whenever molecular descriptors and curated negatives were available \citep{cortes1995svm,breiman2001random,liu2015credible}. Differentiable neural encoders later shifted the field toward learned representations: convolutional and recurrent models over SMILES and protein sequences \citep{ozturk2018deepdta,lee2019deepconv,karimi2019deepaffinity}, and graph neural networks over molecular topology and protein contact maps \citep{nguyen2021graphdta,jiang2020dgraphdta,yang2022mgraphdta}. Attention-based interaction modules sharpened pair-specific matching, from sequence self-attention to bilinear and bidirectional designs that introduce explicit pairwise heads in place of late concatenation \citep{chen2020transformercpi,huang2021moltrans,cheng2022iifdti,bai2023drugban,peng2024bindti}. The most recent wave combines pretrained molecular and protein language models with graph topology and three-dimensional geometric encoders, treating DTI as a multi-view problem that jointly draws on sequence semantics, molecular topology, and conformer geometry \citep{chithrananda2020chemberta,elnaggar2021prottrans,satorras2021egnn,ji2026ldmdti,luo2024druglamp,liu2024geformerdta,shui2025bimadti,zhao2025evidti}. Across this progression, representations have moved from hand-crafted to learned and from unimodal to multimodal, while interaction modeling has moved from concatenation to explicit cross-modal alignment.

Two design questions remain only partially answered within this trajectory. The first concerns how three-dimensional ligand geometry should enter a multimodal pipeline. Most existing systems treat candidate conformers as a target-agnostic property of the drug, although whether a particular conformer is informative typically depends on the protein context with which the ligand is paired \citep{satorras2021egnn,ji2026ldmdti}. The second concerns where long-range sequence modeling should sit relative to cross-modal matching. Structured state-space models offer linear-time long-sequence dynamics that are competitive with self-attention \citep{gu2022s4,gu2023mamba}, and a recent hybrid Mamba-attention design has begun to explore this direction in DTI \citep{shui2025bimadti}; however, applying sequence refinement to mixed drug-protein token streams can blur modality structure, while replacing explicit cross-modal attention may remove the pairwise matching bias that distinguishes DTI from generic representation learning \citep{bai2023drugban,peng2024bindti}. These observations suggest that target-conditioned geometry and protein-side sequential refinement should be introduced carefully and kept separate from the cross-modal matching component.

Motivated by these questions, we propose TAMR-DTI, a target-aware multimodal framework built on a BiIntention-based cross-modal matching backbone \citep{peng2024bindti}. TAMR-DTI introduces a bidirectional drug-target modulation path: protein context first guides conformer-level aggregation so that drug geometry becomes pair-specific, and the resulting drug representation feeds back into protein encoding through a lightweight modulation mechanism. A gated Mamba residual block is then applied only to the ordered protein token stream before BiIntention \citep{gu2023mamba}, using state-space modeling where biological token order is meaningful while preserving the cross-modal matching head as a separate component. The design strengthens target-aware representation learning and long-range protein refinement without imposing artificial sequential dynamics on heterogeneous drug tokens.

The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item We formulate a target-aware modulation strategy that couples conformer aggregation with protein context and conditions protein token encoding on ligand information.
  \item We introduce a protein-side Mamba residual refinement module that improves ordered protein representations while retaining explicit BiIntention-based drug-target matching.
  \item We evaluate TAMR-DTI on BindingDB, BioSNAP, Human, and C. elegans under repeated-seed experimental protocols, showing consistent ranking-metric improvements across all four benchmarks.
  \item We conduct comparative and ablation studies to verify the effectiveness of target-aware modulation and protein-only state-space refinement within a multimodal DTI framework.
\end{itemize}
